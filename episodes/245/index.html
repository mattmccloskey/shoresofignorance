Fact Checks

  • George Bernard Shaw established a trust of money to create a new alphabet, and in the 1960s they produced one called Shavian

    Verified

    Shaw's will funded a prize for a new phonetic alphabet. Kingsley Read's winning design, the Shavian or Shaw Alphabet, was published in 1960, a decade after Shaw's death. Matt's sketch is right. His aside that English uses the Greek alphabet is not: English uses the Latin alphabet, even though that script ultimately descends from Greek, and Shaw's actual complaint was the mismatch between English sounds and English spelling.

  • August Dvorak invented an ergonomic keyboard layout after the typewriter, spent the rest of his life trying to evangelize it, and failed; in the early 2000s someone made a variation called Colemak

    Needs Context

    Dvořák's layout was patented in 1936, and he did spend decades promoting it without displacing QWERTY. Colemak, released by Shai Coleman in 2006, is an ergonomic QWERTY-derived layout influenced by Dvorak, but it is not simply a variation of Dvorak. The early-2000s timing is close enough.

  • The opening of Vanilla Sky was shot by emptying Times Square, with no CGI, after paying New York City about a million dollars, maybe for three or thirty blocks for three hours

    Verified

    Cameron Crowe's crew did shut down Times Square on a Sunday morning in November 2000 and filmed Tom Cruise in a real empty square, not a digital composite. Contemporary accounts put the cost at about a million dollars and the window at roughly three hours, or about ninety minutes in one report. Matt is unsure about the block count, and sources disagree on the exact radius; the only notable digital cleanup in the shot was removing a news ticker that would have dated the film.

  • Vanilla Sky is about 24 years old, and The Matrix came out a few years before it

    Verified

    The Matrix was released in 1999 and Vanilla Sky in December 2001. This episode was recorded in early August 2025, so the film was a few months shy of 24 years old. Close enough, and the relative dating is right.

  • In Vanilla Sky, a company called Life Extension puts the hero in cryogenic suspension with a lucid-dream feature, and after 150 years he has to jump off a building to wake up

    Verified

    That is the plot of the film's third act: David Aames has been in Life Extension's cryonic program, living in a Lucid Dream, and Tech Support tells him it has been 150 years and that the way out is to jump.

  • Texas passed a law this last session that students cannot have phones in schools anymore

    Verified

    Governor Abbott signed House Bill 1481 on June 20, 2025, during the 89th Legislature, requiring school systems to prohibit student use of personal communication devices during the school day. The episode was recorded in early August 2025, after the signing and before the 2025-26 school year, so Michael's timing is right.

  • After World War II, tariffs on the United States were an agreed-upon way for other countries to build back, and that arrangement is now outdated, which is one of the things Trump is renegotiating

    Needs Context

    The postwar order, through Bretton Woods, the Marshall Plan, and GATT, was largely about lowering trade barriers under US leadership, even if Europe and others kept some protection during reconstruction and the United States accepted a degree of asymmetry for a time. Treating tariffs on American goods as the agreed reconstruction policy is a current political shorthand, not a clean history of how those institutions were designed.

  • In The Matrix, Agent Smith says the first versions of the Matrix were rejected, in one case because things were just too good and people kept waking up

    Verified

    In the first film Smith tells Morpheus that the original Matrix was a perfect human world, that it was a disaster, and that no one would accept the program. Matt flags it as a passing line and has the gist right.

  • Everyone falls short of the glory of God, and that is what the law is supposed to show you

    Verified

    Romans 3:23 is the line Michael is quoting, and in Pauline theology the law makes sin known and shows that no one is justified by works. That is the claim they are making about the Old Testament story.

  • Jesus comes and says the law is fulfilled in that no one could accomplish what the law asked for

    Needs Context

    In Matthew 5:17 Jesus says he came to fulfill the law, not abolish it. The further claim that fulfillment means the law proved nobody could keep it is a fair popularization of Paul, especially Romans and Galatians, but it is not a direct quotation of Jesus, and Matt is compressing two biblical voices into one.

  • In the Old Testament, God tells the prophets that their sacrifices are worthless because their hearts are not in the right place

    Verified

    That is a repeated prophetic theme, including Isaiah 1, Amos 5, Hosea 6, Micah 6, and Psalm 51. Michael is summarizing it, not quoting a single verse, and the summary holds.

  • Faith is being sure of what you hope for and certain of what you do not see

    Verified

    That is Hebrews 11:1 in the 1984 NIV wording, which Michael is quoting almost exactly, only changing 'we' to 'you.'

  • Doctor Strange, at the end of Infinity War, evaluates all possible futures

    Verified

    In Avengers: Infinity War, Strange views millions of possible futures and tells Tony he saw only one in which they win. Matt is using the scene as an image, and the reference is right.

  • J.K. Rowling submitted Harry Potter to something like 30 publishers, they all denied her, and she became the most popular writer of all time, second to the Bible

    Incorrect

    The standard account is that about 12 publishers turned down Harry Potter and the Philosopher's Stone before Bloomsbury took it, not 30. The series is among the best-selling in history, but 'the most popular writer of all time, second to the Bible' stacks a loose superlative on a comparison that does not really work, since the Bible is not a modern single-author career.

  • Thomas Sowell said there are no solutions, only trade-offs

    Verified

    This is a well-known Sowell formulation, including from Knowledge and Decisions and later interviews and columns. Matt is quoting it as a cap on the conversation about letting children go.
