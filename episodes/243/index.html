Fact Checks

  • Spotify operated at a loss for years and years, and Uber still operates at a loss

    Needs Context

    Spotify did run at a loss for most of its public life and posted its first full-year profit only in 2024, reported in February 2025, so the historical part is right and Matt already hedges on whether it is still true. Uber is the miss: it reported its first full-year GAAP profit in 2023 and was solidly profitable in 2024, well before this June 2025 recording.

  • Rick Rubin says AI will never be able to do what humans do because humans are not reasonable, and that AI can't invent flight for the Wright brothers

    Verified

    That is the claim in the clip they are discussing, which the episode description also links. Matt is reporting Rubin's point, not inventing it, and he and Michael both take it in the same direction: AI can work with what already is, but it cannot go after what seems impossible.

  • The Wright brothers had to invent, or basically invent, the internal combustion engine to get the plane off the ground

    Incorrect

    The internal combustion engine already existed, including in automobiles. The Wrights could not find a production engine light enough for the power they needed, so their mechanic, Charlie Taylor, designed and built a custom 12-horsepower aluminum-block engine in 1903. Matt even flags that he wants to look it up, and the side-quest framing is fair, but they did not invent the engine itself.

  • You have like a 10% chance statistically of succeeding if you start a small business

    Needs Context

    It depends what 'succeeding' means. U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics figures have long shown that about a fifth of new establishments close in the first year and about half by year five, which is hard, but a 10% success rate, and Michael's '1% of the 1%,' overstate the official failure numbers.

  • Marcus Aurelius wrote Meditations as his journal and did not write it to be published

    Verified

    That is the standard account: the work is a private Greek notebook, often titled To Himself, and was not prepared by Aurelius as a book for readers. Matt is using it as an example of honesty, and the history is right.

  • When somebody wins the lottery, it absolutely destroys them

    Needs Context

    The 'lottery curse' is a real enough pop-culture story, and some winners have gone broke or had their lives unravel, but the research is mixed and does not support a rule that a jackpot 'absolutely' destroys people. Matt is making a moral point about vision, not a careful study of lottery outcomes.

  • In Great Expectations, Pip is about 12, meets an escaped convict in the marsh, later becomes an artist, and a mysterious benefactor tells him he does not have to worry about money anymore and should just do his art

    Incorrect

    The opening is in the right neighborhood: young Pip meets the escaped convict Magwitch in the marshes and is frightened into stealing food and a file, not a canoe. Pip is about seven, not 12, and he does not grow up to be an artist. He is set up as a gentleman of 'great expectations,' and the secret benefactor is Magwitch himself. The lesson Matt wants from the book still works; the plot details do not.

  • In A Moveable Feast, Hemingway and F. Scott Fitzgerald hatch a plan to raise money from their friends so that their friend the poet, T.S. Eliot, can quit his bank job and focus on his poetry, and the plan fails because they were all broke

    Incorrect

    Eliot did work at Lloyds Bank in London, and Hemingway does tell a story in A Moveable Feast about a scheme to get him out of the bank so he could write. The scheme was Ezra Pound's Bel Esprit, with Natalie Barney, not a Hemingway-and-Fitzgerald plan, and it faded after The Waste Land rather than simply because the friends were broke. Eliot left the bank in 1925 for Faber.

  • T.S. Eliot's given names are Thomas Stearns

    Verified

    They are. Matt looks it up late in the episode and has it right.

  • Steve Jobs was fired, or ousted, by the company he started, and if you dropped yourself into his life in 1992 you would find a rich man who felt like a failure

    Needs Context

    Apple forced Jobs out in 1985, not in a voluntary departure, and 1992 falls in the NeXT and Pixar years before his 1997 return, so the imaginative sketch is fair. He was already wealthy, and 'this sucks' is Matt's projection, not a documented 1992 quotation.
