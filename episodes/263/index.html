Fact Checks

  • Scott Adams died, and his ex-wife announced it

    Verified

    Adams died January 13, 2026, at 68, of prostate cancer. His first ex-wife, Shelly Miles, announced it on his livestream and read a statement he wrote on January 1.

  • Adams announced his cancer about six months earlier

    Needs Context

    He announced in May 2025 that he had prostate cancer that had spread to his bones, which was about eight months before his death.

  • Adams backed Trump in 2016 and lost friends and work over it

    Verified

    Adams predicted Trump's win in 2015 and endorsed him during the 2016 campaign. He later wrote that the support cost him speaking work and relationships.

  • Dilbert was canceled after Adams commented on a poll about Black Americans, and over a thousand papers dropped it

    Verified

    In February 2023, Adams reacted to a Rasmussen poll by telling white people to 'get the hell away' from Black people. The New York Times reports that more than 1,000 newspapers dropped the strip.

  • The poll said over half of Black Americans weren't okay with white people

    Needs Context

    Rasmussen asked whether respondents agreed 'It's OK to be white.' 53% of Black respondents agreed, 26% disagreed and 21% were unsure, so just under half didn't agree. Adams based his rant on that 47%.

  • The New York Times and Washington Post obituary headlines called him a disgraced cartoonist

    Incorrect

    People magazine's headline did say 'Disgraced Dilbert Creator.' The Times headline was 'Scott Adams, Creator of the Satirical Dilbert Comic Strip, Dies at 68,' and the Post's was 'Scott Adams, Dilbert creator who poked fun at bad bosses, dies at 68.' Both put the racist-remarks controversy in the article, and the Times also did in its social post.

  • Adams's final statement accepted Christ on a risk-reward basis, and it came near the top

    Verified

    After a note about his estate, the statement says: 'I'm not a believer, but I have to admit the risk-reward calculation for doing so looks attractive. So, here I go: I accept Jesus Christ as my lord and savior.'

  • G.K. Chesterton and C.S. Lewis were both very serious atheists before becoming Christians

    Needs Context

    Lewis was a committed atheist until his conversion in 1929–1931. Chesterton went through a youthful period of skepticism and dabbled in the occult, but he is better described as an agnostic who reasoned his way to orthodoxy than as a serious atheist.

  • Paul says all men know God through what has been made

    Verified

    Romans 1:20: his invisible attributes 'have been clearly perceived… in the things that have been made. So they are without excuse.'

  • How many angels can dance on a pin is a Thomas Aquinas thing

    Needs Context

    Aquinas did ask whether several angels can be in one place (Summa Theologica I, q. 52, a. 3). The 'angels on a pin' phrasing was a later, mostly Protestant and Enlightenment jab at scholasticism and doesn't appear in his work.

  • Bret Weinstein calls religion literally false but metaphorically true

    Verified

    Weinstein has long used the idea of 'metaphorical truth' for beliefs that are literally false but adaptive, which is the framing the hosts debate.

  • Iain McGilchrist argues that all language is metaphorical

    Verified

    In The Master and His Emissary (2009), McGilchrist argues language is grounded in metaphor that links words to bodily experience. Matt's summary is a fair compression.

  • God names himself 'I am that I am'

    Verified

    Exodus 3:14.

  • Paul talks about the renewing of your mind

    Verified

    Romans 12:2: 'Be transformed by the renewal of your mind.'

  • Paul says if this isn't true, we're the biggest fools of all

    Verified

    1 Corinthians 15:19: 'If in Christ we have hope in this life only, we are of all people most to be pitied.' Michael's paraphrase is close.

  • Arranged marriages succeed about as well as, or better than, love marriages

    Needs Context

    Divorce rates are far lower where arranged marriage is common, such as India, but that partly reflects stigma and legal barriers to divorce. Studies of marital satisfaction show mixed results rather than a clear winner.

  • The Robin Williams film with a hell where the ground is made of faces

    Verified

    What Dreams May Come (1998).

  • In The Great Divorce, people in Grey Town keep moving away from each other and Napoleon is unreachable

    Verified

    Lewis (1945) describes the town spreading as residents quarrel and move, and Napoleon living thousands of miles away in a huge house, pacing alone.

  • The Bible says the kingdom of heaven is here and now

    Verified

    Luke 17:21 ('the kingdom of God is in the midst of you') and Jesus's repeated 'the kingdom of heaven is at hand' (Matthew 4:17).

  • In World War II, at the Battle of the Bulge, soldiers stopped for a Christmas truce, and enemy troops played football while waiting

    Incorrect

    The famous Christmas truce, football matches included, was on the Western Front in December 1914, during World War I. The Battle of the Bulge (December 1944) had no such truce, though there are smaller anecdotes like the Vincken family's story from the Hürtgen Forest.

  • Cheryl Hines said The View's hosts were aggressive on camera but friendly when the cameras were off

    Needs Context

    Hines appeared on The View in October 2025 and said she was 'grilled' about RFK Jr. We couldn't confirm the off-camera account Michael describes.

  • Voldemort split his soul into seven pieces

    Verified

    In Harry Potter, Voldemort set out to split his soul into seven parts using Horcruxes.

  • The TV show Lie to Me

    Fox drama (2009–2011) starring Tim Roth as an expert in reading microexpressions.

  • Colonel Jessup's 'You can't handle the truth' is from the Tom Cruise movie A Few Good Men

    Verified

    A Few Good Men (1992). Jack Nicholson plays Col. Jessup opposite Tom Cruise.

  • The Powerball was at a billion recently

  • Statistically, winning the lottery is likely to ruin your life

    Needs Context

    This is a popular belief, but studies (for example Lindqvist and colleagues, 2018) find winners are generally more satisfied with their lives, and bankruptcy risk mostly applies to small wins. Many winners do struggle, but 'likely to ruin your life' overstates it.

  • 'The truth will set you free, but first it'll piss you off' is from Ted Lasso

  • Rich Mullins said God is a wild man, and to hold on, or let go, for dear life
